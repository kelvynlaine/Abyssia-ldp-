Flashcards and spaced repetition: remember for the long term

By Kelvyn Laine, creator of Abyssia · updated October 30, 2026

In short: Spaced repetition means reviewing information at longer and longer intervals, just before you would forget it. Combined with flashcards, which force you to recall the answer from memory, it is one of the most effective ways to remember vocabulary, dates, definitions or formulas for the long term.

Why we forget, and how to stop it

At the end of the 19th century, Hermann Ebbinghaus described the forgetting curve: without review, we lose a large part of new information within a few days. Each review done at the right moment slows that forgetting down. That is the principle of spaced repetition: review a little, but at growing intervals.

Writing good flashcards

  • One idea per card: "When was the Bastille stormed?", not "Summarise the French Revolution".
  • A question that makes you think: the answer should not be guessable from the question.
  • Your own words: rephrasing a definition already helps you understand it.
  • An example when it's abstract: a maths formula sticks better with a worked numerical case.

How often should you review your cards?

A simple schedule before an exam: review a new card the same day, then the next day, three days later, a week later, and so on, stretching the interval each time. A card you get wrong goes back to the start of the cycle. Cards you already know well come up rarely, which leaves time for the hard ones.

Test yourself instead of rereading

The benefit of flashcards comes mostly from the effort of recall: trying to find the answer before reading it. This is known as the testing effect. Say or write the answer before turning the card over, even if you're not sure.
