The Pomodoro technique for studying: a practical guide

By Kelvyn Laine, creator of Abyssia · updated October 30, 2026

In short: The Pomodoro technique means working on a single task for 25 minutes, then taking a 5-minute break; after four rounds, you take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. For revision, it helps you get started, stay focused and see how much work you actually did.

Where does the Pomodoro technique come from?

The Pomodoro technique was developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. It is named after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer (pomodoro is Italian for tomato) that he used as a university student.

How to apply it to your revision

  1. Pick one specific task: "do the chapter 3 quiz" rather than "revise history".
  2. Start a 25-minute timer and work with no phone and no notifications until it rings.
  3. Take a real 5-minute break: stand up, drink some water, don't open social media.
  4. After four "pomodoros", take a long break of 15 to 30 minutes.
  5. Write down what you did in each round: counting sessions is motivating and shows where your time goes.

Choosing what to do in each session

A session is most useful when it makes you produce something: answering a quiz, reciting a flashcard, redoing an exercise without the answer key. Keep rereading for the start of a chapter and move on to testing quickly (see studying with AI in 5 steps).

Example: an evening of revision in four pomodoros

  1. Pomodoro 1: read the chapter notes and write down the three concepts that give you trouble.
  2. Pomodoro 2: take a quiz on the chapter without looking at your notes.
  3. Pomodoro 3: go back over each mistake from the quiz and turn it into a flashcard.
  4. Pomodoro 4: redo a typical exam question, then check it against the answer key.

Then take your long break: you have worked for about two hours, most of it testing yourself rather than rereading.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping breaks: focus drops and the following sessions become less effective.
  • Keeping your phone within reach during the 25 minutes.
  • Choosing a task so vague that you don't know where to start.
  • Sticking to 25 minutes when it doesn't suit you: some people work better in 40- or 50-minute blocks. The principle is alternating work and rest, not the exact number.
