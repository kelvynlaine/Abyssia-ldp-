Revising with a mind map: how to do it

By Kelvyn Laine, creator of Abyssia · updated October 30, 2026

In short: A mind map puts the topic in the middle of the page and organises ideas into branches, with one keyword per branch. It is mainly useful for seeing the structure of a chapter and the links between ideas; to memorise precise definitions or dates, pair it with flashcards or quizzes.

What is a mind map?

A mind map is a diagram that starts from a central idea and branches out into sub-topics, then into details. Instead of a linear list, you get an overview of the whole chapter on a single page.

How to make a mind map in 5 steps

  1. Write the topic in the centre of a sheet of paper turned sideways, inside a box or a small drawing.
  2. Draw the main branches: one for each part of the chapter (causes, consequences, key players, dates…).
  3. Add sub-branches for secondary ideas, with one or two keywords per branch, not sentences.
  4. Use one colour per main branch and a few simple symbols or drawings for the important ideas.
  5. Link related ideas that depend on each other with arrows between branches.

Which subjects is it good for?

  • A great fit: history, geography, economics, biology, philosophy, literature, where you have to connect ideas.
  • Useful for structuring an essay or a presentation before you start writing.
  • Less suited on its own to maths formulas or language vocabulary: you are better off practising with exercises or flashcards.

Redraw it from memory

The best way to use a mind map is to redraw it without looking a few days later, then compare it with the original. The branches you forgot show exactly what you need to review. It's the same principle as testing yourself with a quiz: recalling information from memory fixes it better than rereading it.

Mind map, revision notes or flashcards?

These tools complement each other: the mind map gives you the structure, the revision sheet gathers the details you need to know, and flashcards and quizzes check that everything has stuck.
